### Summary
The paper asks when deep RL (a shared A2C actor, 20k parameters) helps downlink link adaptation once the comparator includes rules calibrated on the learner's own cues. Evidence comes from one MATLAB simulator: a single 10-user cell, SINR-to-BLER abstraction, chase-combining HARQ, PF scheduling, and deadline or full-buffer traffic. The protocol uses disjoint traces, common random numbers, seed-level hierarchical bootstrap, and a public registry of 76 cases. The headline result is that a residual A2C beats OLLA by 7.49 % (after warm-up). That shrinks to +0.66 % over a re-calibrated combined table, and this is not robust across training seeds (hierarchical 95 % CI [-0.30, +1.70] %). A residual on a non-oracle base is reported to beat the combined table by +3.35 % in a pre-registered replication (NR2). On full-buffer goodput the learner adds nothing. The paper also lists three simulation pitfalls and gives measured CPU timing on a laptop.

### Major concerns

1. **Issue** — The paper shows no O-RAN or 5G NR testbed evidence, and the latency and placement analysis does not support any deployment claim. The only measured decision time is a Python/NumPy run on a Windows 10 laptop (i5-8350U), outside any MAC. It shows a median of 180.4 µs, a p99.9 of 632.1 µs, 93.4 % of TTIs within a 60 kHz slot, and 0.0 % at 120 kHz. The recommendation to use a "residual policy on a non-oracle base" is never timed. Fig. 11 times the M1 policy, the oracle-base policy, which the paper itself no longer recommends.
   **Where** — Section VIII-D and Fig. 11; Section VIII-E.2 (no over-the-air validation); Section III (single cell, SINR-to-BLER abstraction).
   **Why it matters** — This is a link-adaptation controller that must act per user and per slot. At 15 kHz, with 10 users per slot, a 180 µs median plus a 632 µs p99.9 for a single user decision is not within a 1 ms budget once all 10 users and the grant-construction path are counted. The p99.9 of one decision alone already exceeds the 500 µs figure the paper cites for LOLLA [5]. The timed path excludes MAC data collection, the HARQ process table, transport to a dApp, and contention with the DU scheduler, which are exactly the costs that dominate in a real stack. The paper's own caveat ("do not establish real-time feasibility") is honest. But the Conclusion, Section VIII-C and the "Where would it run" framing still present a 20k-parameter MLP as cheap, and the Introduction still presents a deployable recipe (Section VII-E). The 40 195 FLOP count is hardware-independent but says nothing about tail latency, which is the quantity that matters here. The paper also gives no placement for the learned residual, which would belong in the DU MAC or a dApp. It never says how the OLLA offset state, the 3-frame history and the busy-HARQ-process features would be exposed to a dApp at slot rate through E3. It also never says how the oracle FixedPU class (true speed class) would be obtained, though the paper stops recommending that base.
   **What would address it** — (a) Time the recommended non-oracle residual policy, in compiled C/C++ or ONNX, inside an OAI or srsRAN MAC or a dApp-style process, with per-slot p50/p99/p99.9 over millions of slots, for 10 users and for a realistic peak load. (b) Report whether the full per-slot loop, including state construction, meets the budget at 30 and 60 kHz. (c) State what feature plumbing and HARQ-state access the policy needs and whether they are available at the DU. (d) Cut the deployment-flavoured language unless these data are added.

2. **Issue** — Testbed and channel realism is too weak to transfer the central ranking (learner vs combined table) to any real RAN. The decisive margin is +0.66 % (+0.40 pp on-time) in M1.
   **Where** — Section III (A–E), Section VIII-E.1; Table II.
   **Why it matters** — The paper's own pitfall (Section VII-C) shows that a PF-averaging definition changed Baseline goodput by 10–26 % and flipped the sign of OLLA vs Baseline. This indicates that conclusions are very sensitive to scheduler and HARQ modelling details. Yet the scheduler gives all 100 RBs to a single user per TTI, with no frequency-selective scheduling, no multi-user MIMO, no CSI-RS or SRS-based CQI, and no real CQI table or reporting periodicity. The "CQI delay 2–8 TTI, Gaussian 1 dB noise" is an abstraction with no support from a measured NR report path. HARQ is modelled as chase combining with a fixed 2.5 dB gain and a fixed 8-TTI RTT with 8 processes. Real NR HARQ timing is TDD pattern dependent and variable (K1/K2 timing, slot pattern DDDSU), and the RTT of 8 is chosen without any grounding in NR numerology (the simulator uses 15 kHz, a regime in which NR defaults are not typical for mid-band TDD). The BLER abstraction has no LDPC validation, and a 1 ms TTI with a 12 000-bit packet every 20 TTIs and a 10-TTI budget is a synthetic traffic model. A +0.66 % effect size and a 1–3 % effect against re-calibrated rules are well within model-mismatch error of the BLER curve and the HARQ combining gain. The "calibrate first" conclusion is plausible, but the claim that the learner's residual is small, or that a +3.35 % non-oracle gain exists, may not survive link-level BLER curves, OAI's OLLA implementation, or a real PF scheduler.
   **What would address it** — (a) Cross-validate the key comparison (non-oracle residual vs combined table vs OLLA) in at least one second environment with link-level LDPC BLER (Sionna, which the paper says exists as a prototype, or the 5G Toolbox / OAI emulation). (b) Sweep the main assumptions (HARQ RTT from a TDD pattern, CQI delay, BLER-curve perturbation, chase gain) and show the sign and rough size of the margin. (c) Preferably, run the OAI/srsRAN port promised in Section VIII-E.2.

3. **Issue (reproducibility lens)** — At review time the paper's reproducibility claims rest on a placeholder. The release is not available, yet the paper's main contribution (C1) is the artefact.
   **Where** — "Code, data and pre-registration availability: [Draft placeholder] Release DOI"; Abstract ("Code, traces and the registry are released"); Section V.D; Table II caption (numbers "checked against" `defaultenv_v17.m`, `v17_setup.m`, which readers cannot see).
   **Why it matters** — The paper repeatedly relies on facts that cannot be verified: hashes of pre-registrations, freeze commits, registry entries, and "generated macros" that supposedly make every number traceable. The abstract states code is released but the availability section says the DOI is a draft placeholder. A pre-registration paper whose pre-registrations and timestamps are not independently accessible, and which was run internally over 17 versions (Aug–Nov 2026), is asking the reader for trust. The pre-registered texts were hashed but apparently not deposited with a third-party timestamping service or registry (OSF, AsPredicted, or a public commit). The pre-registration "from v12 on" is also not a pre-registration of the headline recipe, since the paper admits the recipe was chosen during the campaign.
   **What would address it** — Provide the DOI with an anonymised review archive. Deposit the SHA-256 pre-registration texts in a time-stamped third-party repository. Give a one-command rerun of M1, the combined-table calibration, and NR2 (with the 24.6 h calibration cost noted). Give the Python/NumPy timing code and raw TTI timing data. Reword the Abstract to "will be released" until the artefact exists.

4. **Issue (adversarial)** — The "pre-registered" status of the positive result is weaker than the framing suggests, and the headline positive claim (NR2) is not independent of the selection process.
   **Where** — Abstract; Section V.A and V.D ("We state plainly that the recipe was chosen during the campaign"); Section VI-D.2 (NR1 chosen after being found in a design ablation, NR2 "added later by amendment"); Section VIII-E.6, E.9.
   **Why it matters** — Over 17 versions, 76 cases and many recipe variants (offset vs residual, oracle vs non-oracle base, 800 vs 3000 episodes, two bandits, two tree models), the "recommended" configuration was selected after seeing NR1 and G6 results. NR2 is a genuine fresh-data replication, which is a real strength. However, the 3.35 % NR2 margin is against the combined table that has a documented under-optimisation (one of 4 search starts hit the safety cap), and the paper says "if anything the table is slightly under-optimised, which favours the learner". This is acknowledged but it is a one-sided bias that is not bounded. The paper also treats the oracle-vs-non-oracle swap as a finding when the re-calibrated FixedPU is shown to be only 0.13 % above Fixed. The NR2 M1-oracle policy gap of 2.58 % between non-oracle and oracle residual policies is therefore not explained by information, and headroom analysis (+0.24 points) cannot explain the 2.7-point gap either. It could equally be due to training-seed or recipe variance in the oracle policy, or to reward or initialisation differences between the two. No mechanism is tested ("We did not test this mechanism"). The Bonferroni statement (p = 4.6e-27 survives the factor) is also not meaningful at this scale: the paper's own seed-level analysis shows trace-level p-values massively overstate evidence because traces share the 10 policies, and the cases are not independent tests.
   **What would address it** — Report the NR2 comparison against a more thoroughly optimised combined table (multiple restarts to convergence, a bigger budget), and against a non-oracle combined table (no speed class), since the oracle table is the weaker, partly mis-specified comparator for a non-oracle learner. Add an ablation separating "non-oracle base" from "different base offset value (-2 CQI steps)" and from seed/recipe variance. Do not present trace-level p-values of 1e-27 as evidence.

5. **Issue** — Comparator strength and fairness are not settled, and the literature baselines the paper argues against are not re-implemented.
   **Where** — Section VIII-E.11 ("No learned LA scheme from the literature (for example LOLLA or SALAD) was re-implemented"); Table I; Section V.B (bandit comparators with 41.80 % on-time).
   **Why it matters** — The paper's thesis is that earlier RL-for-LA papers compared against weak baselines. It supports this with a table of 24 papers "screened from abstracts and accessible text", of which 8 are "unclear", and with a within-simulator comparison only. The two contextual bandits perform at 41.80 % versus 52.53 % for the open-loop baseline in M1 (Table V). That means the bandit comparators are broken or severely under-tuned, and so they inflate "beats all nine comparators" counts and add nothing. A reviewer cannot tell whether the nine comparators are a meaningful set. The OLLA used has a fixed 0.5 dB step and a 10 % target, no per-MCS or per-user variants beyond a fast/slow blend, and the SALAD step-size variants and the rank-dependent OLLA of Ahsen et al. [17] are not included. Table I's "Parity" column is based on subjective labels.
   **What would address it** — Fix or drop the bandits. Implement at least one published OLLA variant (adaptive step size, e.g. SALAD [18]) and one published learned scheme (LOLLA-style learning on OLLA) as comparators, or soften the claims about the literature. Move the 24-paper screening protocol (search strings, inclusion criteria, coder agreement) into a supplement so it is reproducible.

6. **Issue** — The effect that is claimed as the "positive case" is small, confined to one regime, and not shown at the operating-point or traffic variety needed for a general answer to "when does deep RL help".
   **Where** — Abstract; Sections VI-D.3, VI-E, VIII-E.5; Tables VI, VII.
   **Why it matters** — The title promises a general answer, but the evidence is one scenario family: 10 users, one cell, one deadline traffic type, two neighbouring load points (only 5 seeds and 300 traces each, with the combined table not in the pre-registered family there), and the original M1 recipe failed at both neighbouring points. With the original recipe the claim "does not generalise to neighbouring operating points" is itself a major negative. The G3 point is at the 0.91 on-time ceiling. Mobility is only a 3–30 m/s range with no pedestrian regime, even though the slow-user (<10 m/s) result is already negative (-1.71 %). The speed breakdown was never repeated for the recommended non-oracle policy. Only 150 scored TTIs per trace with a 50-TTI burn-in, and OLLA starting cold, matters for a loop whose convergence time is the issue; the abstract quotes 7.49 % after a 200-TTI warm-up, but the warm-up check is not the main protocol.
   **What would address it** — Add the speed breakdown, a combined-table comparison at G2/G3, and more seeds for the non-oracle policy. Include a pedestrian-speed regime and a higher user count, and bursty or mixed traffic. Narrow the title and abstract to "in this simulator, for deadline traffic at HARQ RTT 8".

7. **Issue** — Non-stationarity, online training cost and safety: the paper treats the trained policy as a frozen table-like object, and ignores what a RAN operator would need to run it.
   **Where** — Section IV.C; Section VIII-C (Cost); absent elsewhere.
   **Why it matters** — Policies are trained for 3000 episodes per seed over ten seeds, and the ten trained policies differ widely (share of upward corrections ranges from 11 % to 78 %). The paper does not analyse robustness to a distribution shift (new cell, new speeds, a different BLER curve), retraining cadence, or failure safety, such as a bound on BLER excursions when the residual is wrong. Policies that differ so widely across seeds but score nearly the same are a deployment-validation problem (which one to deploy?). The paper itself says a practitioner "has roughly even odds" for the original policy, but this concern is only answered for NR2 with 9 of 10 positive seeds, a result not reported with per-seed values for NR1.
   **What would address it** — Evaluate a trained policy under channel/BLER mismatch, report the BLER and retransmission distributions for the policy vs the combined table, and propose a deployment procedure (seed selection on validation traces, guardrails such as clipping the residual to ±1 CQI, which the policy already does).

### Minor concerns
- The abstract is dense, overloaded with qualifiers and numbers, and hard to parse; the "key" result (the non-oracle residual) is described several times in different words across the Abstract, Introduction and Conclusion.
- Margins are expressed in relative percent (+0.66 %) while the corresponding absolute change is +0.40 pp; use pp as primary since on-time ratio is a probability and the denominators change.
- The "Bonferroni factor 972 = 108 × 9" and later "at most 1 260 tests" give numbers that are inconsistent with the "67 cases / 108 evaluations" and "76 cases / 126 evaluations" without a clear table; p = 1.00 after correction is presented as informative but is an artefact of an arbitrary family definition.
- The paper says "7 cases (18 evaluations) were superseded" and "9 of the 60 valid cases show a WIN", but the 60 / 67 / 76 / 49 case counts are hard to track; add a single reconciliation table.
- Table X: the "Beats" column shows yes for Bandit (global) at +1.32 % identical to OLLA; such coincidences (identical bandit rows, tuned-target OLLA equal to OLLA) suggest degenerate comparators and should be explained or removed.
- Table II lists "Speed range (Doppler) 3–30 m/s (20–200 Hz)": at 2 GHz, 3 m/s is about 20 Hz and 30 m/s about 200 Hz, consistent; but 30 m/s with 15 kHz is a highway scenario and a speed breakdown for pedestrian speeds is absent.
- The 40 195 FLOP count is stated as 2 x 19 872 + 451; give the breakdown, and note it does not include the state-construction time that dominates the measured 180 µs.
- Fig. 11 reports the wall-clock for Python/NumPy; a per-user versus per-TTI definition should be explicit ("for 10 users" vs "per decision").
- The statement "a deterministic ensemble reaches 0.6306" is intriguing (it exceeds the single-policy 0.6126 by far); if an ensemble removes seed variance and gives +2 pp, this suggests the robust form of the learner and deserves to be in the pre-registered analysis, not a footnote.
- The "AI-assistance disclosure" and funding sections are placeholders; author names are "to be completed". Citation [30] is used for tuning-budget reporting, which is a stretch for recommender systems.
- Section II.B says LOLLA treats uplink with an immediate CRC outcome; the paper then cites the LOLLA observation ablation numbers (254, 185, 171 Mbps) as evidence of observation-set dependence while those are from a different setting.
- Fig. 4 and Fig. 6 omit bandits "for scale"; consider a log axis instead of hiding comparators.

### Verdict
**Major revision** — The calibrated-comparator methodology and the seed-level honesty are valuable and the conclusion "calibrate first" is credible, but without any measured in-MAC/dApp latency for the recommended policy, a second environment with link-level BLER or testbed evidence, and a released artefact (currently a placeholder), the transferable and deployment-relevant claims, including the +3.35 % non-oracle gain, are not adequately supported for this venue.
