# Review by reviewer alfa

**Paper:** "When Does Deep Reinforcement Learning Help Link Adaptation? A Pre-Registered Evaluation Against Calibrated Rules"
**Target venue:** IEEE Transactions on Machine Learning in Communications and Networking

### Summary

The paper asks how much of a deep-RL link-adaptation gain over OLLA survives once the comparator is a rule calibrated on the learner's own cues. In a single MATLAB simulator (10 users, one scheduled per TTI, SINR-to-BLER abstraction, multi-process HARQ), a shared A2C policy (primary scheme: a residual policy of ±1 CQI step on top of a calibrated base rule) is compared with nine comparators. For deadline traffic at RTT 8 the policy beats cold-start OLLA by 11.96 % (7.49 % after a 200-TTI warm-up). Against rules re-calibrated on 500 traces the margin falls to +1.43 % (Context table) and +0.66 % (a 36-cell "combined" table). The latter margin is not robust across the ten training seeds (hierarchical 95 % CI [−0.30, +1.70] %). A non-oracle residual variant (NR1, replicated as NR2) beats the combined table by +3.35 % (CI [2.22, 4.40] %). On full-buffer goodput the learner adds nothing (−0.88 %, −1.73 % against Fixed). A "headroom" analysis, three simulation pitfalls and a public case registry complete the study.

**Steelman.** The strongest version of the argument is a methodological paper, not a "RL wins" paper. Gains of learned link adaptation over OLLA are largely a calibration artefact. A same-information comparator, CRN-paired disjoint traces, seed-level intervals and a registry of every case (including failures and superseded results) are the right way to study this, and the paper does unusually well at reporting its own negative and withdrawn results. I think this contribution is real and valuable, and the paper's candour is its best feature. I press on whether the statistical machinery and the wording of the conclusions actually support what is claimed. In several places they do not.

### Major concerns

1. **Issue — The headline "calibrate first / the learner adds a fraction of a point" conclusion depends on an estimand chosen to be pessimistic, and the paper's own secondary result contradicts it.**
   - **Where:** Sec. IV-C ("primary scheme is the per-seed mean"), Sec. VI-C (last paragraph: deterministic ensemble reaches 0.6306 on time), Sec. VIII-B.
   - **Why it matters:** The per-seed-mean policy scores 0.6126 against 0.6086 for the combined table (+0.66 %). The ten-seed deterministic ensemble scores 0.6306, i.e. about +3.6 % over the combined table, more than five times the headline margin. It is also about +2.9 % over the per-seed mean, which is far outside the hierarchical CI that is said to define what a practitioner can expect. The ensemble is waved away as "not part of the pre-registered decision", but nothing in the stated question ("when does RL help") restricts the deployable artefact to a single seed. The per-seed mean answers "what does a practitioner who trains one policy get", and the ensemble (10x inference cost, 10 x 3000 episodes of training) answers a different question. The conclusion "the learned residual then adds a fraction of a percentage point" is therefore a statement about one estimand only. The large seed heterogeneity (upward-step share 11–78 % across seeds with near-equal on-time ratio) suggests a variance problem of the training procedure, not an absence of learnable structure. The paper conflates the two.
   - **What would address it:** Report the ensemble (and, e.g., best-of-N on validation traces, or a distilled/averaged policy) as pre-specified deployable variants with trace- and seed-level intervals. The seed-level interval for an ensemble requires multiple independent ensembles (for example, several disjoint 5-seed ensembles from additional training runs). Qualify the abstract and conclusion to say that the result concerns single-seed deployment.

2. **Issue — The seed-level inference is under-powered and its calibration is unestablished, yet it is the paper's decisive "robustness" criterion.**
   - **Where:** Sec. V-C (Table IV, rules R1/R2), Sec. VI-C, Table VI, Fig. 5, NR-G10 (5 seeds), Table VII.
   - **Why it matters:** (a) A hierarchical (seed × trace) percentile bootstrap with 10 top-level clusters (5 in NR-G10) is known to be anti-conservative. With so few clusters, percentile intervals under-cover and the two-level resampling does not repair this, since the seed level has only 9 degrees of freedom. The [2.22, 4.40] % and [0.90, 2.73] % intervals should be treated as optimistic. (b) No power analysis is given, although [35] is cited. "5 of 10 seeds beat the table" with a sign test p = 0.623 is simply an inconclusive test, and non-significance at ten seeds cannot establish "not robust" as a property of the method; it is equally consistent with a small positive effect. The paper's wording slips between "cannot be shown" and "not real" ("whether the remaining gain is real… it is not", Sec. I, contribution 2). (c) R1 uses a 90 % two-sided t-interval (and a Holm-adjusted two-sided Wilcoxon at 0.05) while R2 uses a 95 % interval. The two rules sit at different effective error levels (a one-sided 5 % bound against a one-sided 2.5 % bound), so the "beats on traces but not seed-robust" classification partly reflects the confidence-level mismatch (the combined-table lower bound at trace level is +0.29, at seed level −0.30). (d) The probability of improvement of 0.55 pools seeds and traces and has a CI of [0.49, 0.61]. Its interpretation as "close to a coin flip" is reasonable, but it is dominated by trace noise, not seed noise.
   - **What would address it:** Use a proper seed-level analysis. Examples: a t- or BCa interval on the ten per-seed means, a permutation/randomisation test, or a mixed model with seed and trace random effects. Align the confidence levels of R1 and R2. Add a simulation (or a plain power calculation from the observed seed SD) showing the minimal detectable seed-level margin at n = 10 and 5. Increase the seed count for the decisive comparisons (30+ seeds is cheap at 2.7 h for ten) and reword "not robust" to "not established".

3. **Issue — Trace-level p-values and the project-wide multiplicity accounting are not meaningful as reported.**
   - **Where:** Abstract/C1 (p = 4.6×10⁻²⁷ "after the project-wide Bonferroni factor"), Sec. V-D, Tables V, VI, IX–XI.
   - **Why it matters:** The Wilcoxon p-values treat the 500 traces as the only source of randomness and condition on the particular trained policies (ten seeds) and on the particular calibration. They quantify the evaluation-trace sampling error of a fixed policy set, not the sampling error of the method. A p of 10⁻²⁷ is therefore a statement about a quantity the authors themselves say is not what a practitioner experiences. Quoting it in the contributions list invites over-reading. Further: (i) The correction factors are inconsistent: Bonferroni factor 972 = 108 × 9 in one place and "at most 1 260 tests" = 126 × 10 in another. The abstract says "all 76 cases are registered" while the registry has 67 cases and the other 9 sit in a "ledger". (ii) Several comparator rows are bit-identical (Bandit global/delay-class; tuned OLLA = OLLA; FixedPU = 0.00 in Table X; Context = Fixed in Tables X/XI), so the nominal family size is inflated by duplicates and the "beats all nine" language is partly empty. (iii) Table XV counts 10 WIN cases while the text says 9 of 60 valid cases show a WIN; the relation should be stated. (iv) The multiplicity framework counts cases and schemes but does not model the real forking path. The recipe, base-rule selector and comparator family were chosen across v10–v16 after seeing the evaluation outcomes, so a Bonferroni factor over executed tests understates selection.
   - **What would address it:** Drop p-values below about 10⁻⁴ from the abstract/contributions. Report seed-level effect sizes as the primary evidence. Reconcile the counts (67/76 cases; 108/126 evaluations; 972 vs 1 260; 9 vs 10 wins) in one table. Remove duplicate comparators from the multiplicity family and say so.

4. **Issue — "Pre-registered" and "confirmatory" are stronger than the design supports.**
   - **Where:** Title, Abstract, Sec. V-D ("the recipe was chosen during the campaign; M1 is a confirmatory test of that recipe on fresh data"), Sec. VI-D2–3, Limitation 9, Appendix D (Table XIV).
   - **Why it matters:** The residual recipe, the oracle base rule, the 3000-episode budget and the reward shaping evolved over 17 versions on overlapping scenarios. M1 is the v17 main round and is "confirmatory" only against fresh traces of the same simulator and the same scenario family. The decisive finding (NR1/NR2) was a post hoc design change motivated by the failure of the pre-registered M1 recipe, and NR2 itself "was added after the other results were known" by amendment. The operating-point check (G10) was negative for the M1 recipe, the recipe was then changed, and the re-run NR-G10 was positive, so this is a classic forking path. At the neighbouring points the strongest comparator was dropped from the pre-registered family "for compute reasons" (Amendment 2), and the descriptive per-cell combined table was computed with a lighter search (200 traces instead of 500). Seed counts differ (5 vs 10). Registrations that fix wording and decision rules but no hypotheses with prespecified effect sizes or power make rejection easy to satisfy. Pre-registration with wording-only decisions is fine, but it is not equivalent to confirmatory evidence when the hypothesis was selected from the observed data.
   - **What would address it:** Retitle or qualify ("a documented, partially pre-registered evaluation"). State in the abstract which results are confirmatory (NR2 vs the frozen table) and which are exploratory (everything chosen on M1/NR1). Do not claim robustness at neighbouring points against "the table" until the full combined-table comparison, at 500 calibration traces and 10 seeds, has been run there. Report the number of design variants tried between v10 and NR1, so a reader can judge selection.

5. **Issue — The "58 % removed by re-calibration" claim attributes to calibration an effect that is mostly due to adding a different, richer comparator.**
   - **Where:** Abstract; Sec. VI-B; Sec. VIII-B ("Calibrate first").
   - **Why it matters:** The margin over the Context table barely moves with re-calibration (+1.58 → +1.43 %, about 9 %). The remaining drop to +0.66 comes from introducing a new rule (the 36-cell combined table over slack × delay × speed × busy-HARQ), which also uses the oracle speed class. This is a change of function class, not calibration. The "calibrate first" slogan therefore mixes two lessons (tune the baseline; give the baseline the same information) and the 58 % figure supports only the second. In addition, 15 of the 36 combined-table cells (all marked * in Table XII, including most of the tight-slack cells where the learner's action frequency is highest) are not calibrated at all but are filled by a hand-built composite rule from single-cue tables, and one of four coordinate-ascent starts hit the sweep cap while still improving. The "fully calibrated" characterisation is therefore overstated. The authors argue that under-optimisation favours the learner, which is true for the M1 sign but also means the table's margin is an upper bound on a true best table, and the +0.66 % is not a calibrated-table-ceiling estimate. Conversely, the learner's own hyper-parameters and recipe have had 17 rounds of tuning on this simulator, while the comparator got a single 24.6 h search; the budgets are not symmetric in the number of researcher design iterations, only in simulator hours.
   - **What would address it:** Separate the two effects in the text and in the "58 %" figure. Report a calibrated richer-table ablation (e.g., different binnings or a decision-tree/GBT policy fitted on the calibration traces) to show where the table ceiling lies. State and, if possible, equalise the tuning effort (including design iterations) for learner and comparators.

6. **Issue — The headroom analysis (HR1) is over-interpreted and is not an independent statistical test of the claims it is used to support.**
   - **Where:** Sec. VI-G, Fig. 9, Abstract ("one-step room over every table, even on goodput, so the missing gain there is not due to a lack of room"), C3, Conclusion.
   - **Why it matters:** The headroom is computed around the logged decisions of each learned policy, from cross-fitted gradient-boosted trees that predict myopic expected useful bits of the first transmission. (i) The state distribution is generated by the policy under study (and by its base rule), so the headroom is conditional on that policy's visited states. (ii) The estimand is first-transmission bits, whereas the margins are on-time ratio or goodput over whole traces including HARQ, queueing and PF coupling. The paper admits the units differ and compares "qualitatively", but the abstract states a firm causal conclusion. (iii) The headroom of 2.38 % for goodput is a predictor-dependent estimate (gbt_b is lower throughout, as stated) and relies on a model that has access to features the table lacks, so a positive headroom over a table is nearly guaranteed with any flexible predictor and says little about learnability by an RL agent. (iv) The statement that "headroom around the non-oracle base is larger by 0.24 points [0.11, 0.38]" is used to explain a 2.7-point realised difference. By the authors' own numbers, 0.24 vs 2.7 means HR1 explains under 10 % of what it is cited to explain. (v) The headroom analysis does not include the obvious practical comparator, the one-step supervised GBT policy itself, evaluated end to end in the simulator.
   - **What would address it:** Remove the causal sentence from the abstract. Evaluate the GBT one-step predictor as an actual scheme in M1/M3 (a non-RL "learned table" comparator), which tests whether the claimed headroom is realisable. Report headroom around the base rule's own decision distribution, not only around the learner's.

7. **Issue — Comparator and baseline quality, and the evaluation unit, limit what "beats all comparators" can mean.**
   - **Where:** Table V (Bandit global/delay class at 41.80 % on time, i.e. 46.55 % margin and 13 points below OLLA), Tables IX–XI, Sec. IV-A/III-E (150 scored TTIs, about 7 packets per user per trace), Sec. V-C.
   - **Why it matters:** Two contextual-bandit comparators perform far worse than the open-loop Baseline (52.5 %). That indicates an implementation or design problem, not a competitive learning baseline, and they are the only learned baselines in the study. The paper's own thesis (weak baselines inflate RL gains) applies to them. The claim "beats all nine comparators" counts them. No learned LA scheme from the literature ([2], [5], [18]) is reproduced (acknowledged in Limitation 11). On the evaluation unit, traces are 150 scored TTIs with 10 users and one PF-scheduled user per TTI; per-trace on-time ratios are averages over only a few dozen packets, so the per-trace differences are coarse and non-normal, which is questionable for the t-interval used in R1 (the Wilcoxon test and the t-interval answer different questions: median-like shifts versus the mean margin). The same 500 evaluation traces are reused for the main comparison, warm-up, G6, NR1, HR1 and the speed breakdown, so every analysis after M1 is adaptive over the same data (only NR2 and NR-G10 use fresh traces).
   - **What would address it:** Either repair the bandits (compare with the tuning-free formulation of [2] or [19]) or remove them from "beats all". Report bootstrap rather than t intervals for the trace-level margin (or justify normality). Quantify what fraction of the per-trace variance sits in traces vs users.

8. **Issue — Unexplained and statistically fragile subgroup and replication findings that the recommendation rests on.**
   - **Where:** Sec. VI-D2 (NR1/NR2), Sec. VI-F2 (speed breakdown), Sec. VIII-B, Limitations 4 and 6.
   - **Why it matters:** The central recommendation is that a residual on a non-oracle base beats the combined table by +3.35 %, yet the oracle-base residual scored only +0.66 %, although the base rules themselves differ by only 0.13 % (Sec. VIII-B) and the headroom analysis explains 0.24 points of the 2.7-point gap. A 2.7-point difference between two nominally identical training recipes (differing in one base offset table of near-equal quality) is much larger than the within-recipe seed spread, and no mechanism is established ("HR1 does not test how"). Seed-to-seed variation within a recipe is large (action shares 11–78 %), so a difference between two sets of ten seeds could be a recipe-level lottery (a few "good" seeds in the NR set), notably as NR1 reported +2.44 % and NR2 +3.35 % against the same table, which is not consistent with winner's-curse shrinkage. The seed-level numbers for NR1 were not stored. The slow-user loss (−1.71 %, CI [−3.32, −0.15]) was found in a post hoc subgroup analysis against the original, not the re-calibrated, comparator and has not been repeated for the recommended policy, yet it is listed as one of three headline qualifications. The recommended configuration may or may not share it.
   - **What would address it:** Add a third independent set of seeds for the non-oracle and oracle recipes and report the between-recipe difference directly with a seed-level interval. Report the speed breakdown against the combined table for NR2. Either test a mechanism (e.g., swap the base tables, freeze the residual) or soften the recommendation.

9. **Issue — External validity of a single, abstract simulator and a very short horizon.**
   - **Where:** Sec. III (SINR-to-BLER abstraction, one cell, 10 users, one user per TTI, 150 scored TTIs), Sec. VIII-E.
   - **Why it matters:** The pitfalls section itself shows that conclusions flipped with a PF-averaging definition and with a mobility-time-scale error, which indicates strong sensitivity to simulator details. All findings come from one MATLAB code base with no cross-validation (acknowledged). A 150 ms scoring window is much shorter than an OLLA convergence time at BLER target 0.1 (hence the large cold-start effect, which the paper itself shows). The learned policy's value is also tied to deadline traffic parameters at one load. The generalisation claims in the title ("When does deep RL help link adaptation?") are broader than a single deadline-traffic operating point and two neighbours. The cost comparison (Fig. 11) is measured in Python/NumPy on a laptop and is, as the authors say, not a feasibility result.
   - **What would address it:** Narrow the title and abstract to the simulated setting, or add at least one independent environment (e.g., the released Sionna prototype, with link-level BLER) in which the key comparison (non-oracle residual vs a calibrated combined table) is replicated. Extend the scoring window or add the warm-up variant to every comparison.

10. **Issue — Reproducibility and disclosures are placeholders in the submitted text.**
    - **Where:** "Code, data and pre-registration availability" ("[Draft placeholder] Release DOI"), Acknowledgments (AI-assistance disclosure placeholder, reference to an internal `submission_checklist.md`), author names.
    - **Why it matters:** Reproducibility and the pre-registration hashes are central claims. The reviewer cannot verify the registry, hashes, freeze dates or the claims ledger. The text also uses generated macros and a claims ledger, which is good practice, but the reviewer can only see the final numbers.
    - **What would address it:** Provide the DOI/anonymised repository with frozen registration files and hashes at submission. Complete the funding and AI-assistance statements.

### Minor concerns

- The abstract is overloaded (about 20 numbers, three operating points, three pitfalls). Please lead with the single decisive result and its CI. "7.49 % more packets" is a relative figure; state absolute percentage points next to it (as is done for 0.40 pp in the text).
- Table I (and the "0/3/13/8" screening counts) rest on abstracts and "accessible text". The screening protocol (search, inclusion criteria, who coded, agreement) is not described, and only 8 of 24 papers are shown. As stated, the "0 of 24" headline cannot be audited. Wording such as "the literature suggests" should be softened accordingly.
- Sec. VI-D3 is internally contradictory as written: it states that the check "was negative … at the two tested neighbouring points it was not reproduced", and then that "the gain held" for the non-oracle recipe, with near-duplicate sentences repeated in Limitation 5. Rewrite this passage once, clearly.
- The 5-seed, 300-trace NR-G10 intervals are described as "indicative" yet are listed as "robust" in Table VII. Mark them as such in the table.
- Fig. 4 and Table V use "Holm p" over comparator families that include duplicated comparators. Table XI lists OLLA-tuned at CI [−1.50, −0.30] with Holm p = 8.2×10⁻², which is a nice illustration of the Holm/CI mismatch. Explain this.
- The 90 % interval for traces (R1) versus the 95 % interval for seeds (R2) should be harmonised (see Major 2) or the choice justified in Table IV.
- "Hierarchical intervals are about 2.3 times wider" is an average; please give the per-comparator inflation factors.
- Table II caption and Sec. III refer to internal file names (DefaultEnv_v17, v17_setup.m) and Sec. V to "v9–v17"; readers do not know these versions. Describe versions in the supplement or drop the version nomenclature from the main text.
- References: [26] "IEEE Transactions on Networking" appears to be a mis-citation (IEEE/ACM Transactions on Networking?); [45] has no venue; [32] (SIGCOMM 2026 proceedings) and [4], [7], [18] are preprints; please check each entry. The classic evaluation-methodology literature for RL benchmarking is cited, but there is no discussion of prior residual-RL-on-a-hand-designed-controller work, which would clarify the novelty of the "residual on a calibrated base" recipe (it is presented as a finding of the campaign).
- The statement "the true speed class is worth almost nothing" (+0.13 %) is derived from a single trace set; give an interval.
- Fig. 9 mixes headroom in expected useful bits with margins in on-time ratio on the same axis; consider separate panels.
- Figures 3–6 and 8 are small; the 12 figures and 15 tables could be reduced (Figs. 2 and 12, for example, are protocol flow charts with little information).
- The Jain-index guard (drop above 0.02, edge5 drop above 10 %) is stated, but the thresholds are not motivated.

### Verdict

**Major revision** — The paper makes a valuable methodological point and reports honestly, but the decisive statistical claims (seed-level non-robustness, the pre-registered/confirmatory status, the 58 % re-calibration effect and the headroom explanation) rest on under-powered seed-level inference, post hoc design selection and an estimand (single-seed mean) that the authors' own ensemble result contradicts; these need re-analysis and re-wording before it is publishable.
