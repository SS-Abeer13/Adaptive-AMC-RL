### Summary
**Steelman:** This report provides a rare and commendable example of empirical honesty in the reinforcement learning for wireless communications literature. Rather than papering over a clean 0-for-9 null result on a pre-registered benchmark against an oracle-calibrated constant offset rule (`FixedPU`), the authors methodically dissect the common claim that deep RL improves link adaptation by learning sophisticated, state-conditional channel-tracking policies. Across a 12-point campaign, state-information ablations, and an OLLA target-BLER sweep, they provide convincing evidence that deep RL's apparent +7–13% advantage over the standard Outer Loop Link Adaptation (OLLA) loop is primarily a setpoint artifact: operating at an aggressive first-transmission BLER (~40–45%) that exploits HARQ chase combining—a scalar shift that requires no dynamic state tracking and is matched by a static constant offset.
**The Press:** Despite this commendable transparency, the paper's overarching conclusion—that "the optimal link-adaptation policy in this environment class is a constant, not a state-conditional function"—collapses under scrutiny because its comparator baseline suite is severely flawed and under-tuned. First, the paper's headline advantage over OLLA is an artifact of evaluating on cold-start transients: when OLLA is evaluated at steady state with a 1000-TTI warm-up (`Warm OLLA`), OLLA actually *defeats* Deep A2C by 5.9% ($p = 0.0003$), an empirical reversal the authors dismiss as a "handicap" rather than confronting as a fatal confound. Second, OLLA's target-BLER sweep is crippled by static step sizes that leave measured BLER stuck at ~0.65 across all targets, testing a saturated baseline rather than a tuned one. Third, the authors equate "state-conditional adaptivity" with a memoryless 2-layer MLP operating on delayed, noisy feedback, completely omitting standard dynamic baselines from communications engineering (e.g., multi-offset OLLA [arXiv:2604.20459], channel-prediction filters, or contextual bandits [arXiv:1708.00994]). The authors have not shown that the environment lacks adaptivity value; they have shown that an under-specified feedforward RL agent fails to beat a simple constant while steady-state classical control actually beats the agent.

### Major concerns

1. **Issue:** `[Narrative — overclaiming]` The abstract and introduction claim that RL "recovers" oracle-calibrated constant performance without calibration data, but parity was achieved only through behavioral cloning on the oracle's actions, while the from-scratch learner suffered a 5.1% throughput deficit.
   **Where:** Abstract (lines 20–22), Section 6 (lines 413–423), Section 12 (lines 621–623, 633–636).
   **Why it matters:** A reader of the abstract would conclude that Deep A2C independently discovered the oracle's optimal operating point purely through environment interactions. In reality, `tier1b` achieved statistical parity with `FixedPU` ($p = 0.813$) only because its weights were explicitly initialized via behavior cloning directly on the oracle's action distribution, after which 500 episodes of RL fine-tuning produced exactly zero additional throughput. The only methodologically clean from-scratch learner (`tier1-nobc`) suffered a 5.1% throughput deficit (3.54 vs. 3.66 Mbps). Presenting this as "recovering oracle performance without calibration" conflates copying an oracle with learning autonomously.
   **What would address it:** Rewrite the abstract and introduction to state clearly that pure RL from scratch trailed the oracle constant by 5.1%, and that statistical parity was attained strictly by cloning the oracle comparator.

2. **Issue:** `[Novelty — missing prior art and literature attribution]` The report frames the finding that HARQ chase combining favors a ~40–45% initial BLER as an empirical RL discovery, ignoring extensive prior literature in wireless communications establishing optimal HARQ operating points, as well as prior RL formulations for outer-loop adaptation.
   **Where:** Section 8 (lines 497–509), Section 11 (lines 590–597).
   **Why it matters:** The central physical mechanism identified in the paper—that chase combining provides +2.5 dB per retransmission, subsidizing aggressive first transmissions and shifting optimal BLER from 10% to 30–50%—is standard cellular communications theory established since LTE Release 8. Presenting this as a novel empirical insight from RL obscures decades of existing PHY/MAC link-adaptation literature. Furthermore, existing work that explicitly frames outer-loop link adaptation as reinforcement learning (e.g., Pulliyakode & Kalyani [arXiv:1708.00994]) and self-adaptive link adaptation tracking SINR from ACK/NACK feedback (e.g., SALAD, Wiesmayr et al. [arXiv:2510.05784]) are completely uncited.
   **What would address it:** Properly attribute the HARQ BLER operating-point trade-off to prior wireless communications literature, cite foundational RL-for-OLLA work [arXiv:1708.00994] and modern feedback-tracking link adaptation [arXiv:2510.05784], and position the paper's contribution specifically as a red-team evaluation rather than a discovery of HARQ economics.

3. **Issue:** `[Evidence — distinguishing test]` The Tier-A vs. Tier-B state ablation fails to differentiate between the absence of physical adaptivity value and the inability of a small, memoryless feedforward network to learn from delayed, noisy signals within 500 episodes.
   **Where:** Section 2.5 (lines 214–221), Section 7 (lines 425–432), Figure 6.
   **Why it matters:** The authors treat the statistical indistinguishability of Tier-A (+0.1%) and Tier-B (-0.7%) as proof that the environment contains no exploitable state-conditional structure. However, the learner is a 2-layer (96x96) feedforward MLP without recurrence, tasked with processing 2–8 TTI delayed feedback and noisy binary ACK/NACKs. In partially observable, delayed environments (POMDPs), feedforward policy-gradient networks are notorious for failing to discover effective representations for continuous derivative features (like 4-TTI SINR slope or delayed burst indicators). Showing that an undertrained feedforward network extracts zero value from 5 extra features does not prove the features lack information.
   **What would address it:** Include an expressive sequence baseline (e.g., an LSTM/GRU actor or an explicit autoregressive channel filter) or a supervised regression oracle demonstrating whether Tier-B features actually predict future SINR in this simulation.

4. **Issue:** `[Evidence — alternative explanations]` The observed collapse of the policy to a static action distribution is fully consistent with known optimization failure modes of policy gradients in noisy, delayed POMDPs, rather than evidence that the true optimal policy is static.
   **Where:** Section 10.4 (lines 554–563), Figure 13.
   **Why it matters:** In Section 10.4, the unimodal action histogram of Deep A2C is presented as "direct evidence for the constant-policy thesis." But when an actor-critic network faces high environment noise, delayed credit assignment, and entropy regularization, policy gradients are heavily biased toward collapsing to the marginal expectation (a deterministic constant offset) to minimize variance. The empirical convergence of Deep A2C to a single mode is an expected failure mode of A2C under partial observability, not a mathematical characterization of the MDP's optimal policy.
   **What would address it:** Evaluate the algorithm on a simplified, zero-delay, low-noise control environment where the optimal policy is analytically known to be state-dependent, proving whether this A2C implementation is capable of escaping static mode collapse.

5. **Issue:** `[Evidence — statistical rigor]` The report relies on non-significant p-values to claim "parity" while fresh-seed replication demonstrates severe performance instability.
   **Where:** Section 4.1 (Table 1), Section 4.2 (lines 346–351), Section 6 (lines 413–423).
   **Why it matters:** In Section 6, the authors characterize the -5.1% deficit of `tier1-nobc` as "near-parity" because $p = 0.06$ misses the arbitrary 0.05 significance threshold on a small sample of 30 traces. Failing to reject the null is not evidence of equivalence. More alarmingly, Section 4.2 documents that simply drawing a fresh random seed flipped the headline point from parity (-0.7%, $p = 0.517$) to a statistically significant failure (-4.4%, $p = 0.006$). When seed variation causes 4% performance swings, asserting parity based on $p = 0.06$ on a single seed configuration is statistically unfounded.
   **What would address it:** Perform two-one-sided equivalence tests (TOST) with defined equivalence margins, and report confidence intervals across at least 20–30 random seeds to establish genuine reproducibility.

6. **Issue:** `[Reproducibility — missing training and environment parameters]` Essential hyperparameters, the mathematical specification of the reward function, trace lengths, and code availability are omitted from the report.
   **Where:** Section 2 (lines 164–221), Section 13 (lines 658–720), absent.
   **Why it matters:** The report provides MATLAB code snippets for transmission physics and behavior cloning, but omits the exact reward function (how goodput, delays, and packet drops are mathematically balanced), discount factor $\gamma$, optimizer settings, learning rates, trace length in TTIs, and user count per cell. Furthermore, no repository link or archived code artifact is supplied. An independent researcher cannot replicate the simulation or diagnose the reported undertraining issues without these parameters.
   **What would address it:** Add a dedicated appendix table specifying all MDP parameters ($\gamma$, reward formulation, episode length, learning rates, network activations) and provide a persistent repository link with replication instructions.

7. **Issue:** `[Evidence — baselines]` The paper's claimed advantage over OLLA (+7–13%) is an artifact of evaluating cold-start transients on short traces—since steady-state OLLA actually beats Deep A2C—while the OLLA target sweep is crippled by static step sizes and modern multi-offset baselines are entirely omitted.
   **Where:** Section 3 (lines 224–243), Section 4.1 (Table 1, lines 321–328), Section 8 (Table 3, lines 458–509).
   **Why it matters:** This defect directly compromises the validity of both empirical conclusions in the paper:
   - **Steady-state OLLA beats Deep A2C:** In Table 1, under the `Warm OLLA` condition (1000 TTIs of convergence), OLLA achieves 3.77 Mbps, **beating Deep A2C (3.56 Mbps) by 5.9% ($p = 0.0003$)**. The authors dismiss this result as an "unrealistic 1000-TTI convergence head start." In operational cellular networks, base stations serve active data flows over thousands of TTIs; link adaptation is not torn down every 200 ms. The entire +7–13% gain reported across the other points is an artifact of scoring OLLA during its initial transient integration phase from an arbitrary initialization.
   - **The OLLA target-BLER sweep is a strawman:** In Table 3, when target BLER is swept from 0.10 to 0.50, OLLA's measured first-transmission BLER remains frozen at ~0.65 across all settings, and throughput remains flat. In classical OLLA, the target BLER is set by the ratio of step sizes ($\Delta_{up} / (\Delta_{up} + \Delta_{down})$). If the measured BLER does not budge when the target parameter is swept, the integrator is structurally saturated or step sizes are improperly scaled to track Markov interference bursts. Sweeping a broken, saturated integrator does not prove OLLA cannot operate at 40% BLER; it proves the baseline implementation was defective.
   - **Absence of industry-standard adaptive baselines:** In multi-user systems with heterogeneous delays and bursty interference, classical engineering does not deploy a single static scalar OLLA. Standard solutions include multi-offset OLLA (e.g., Ahsen et al. [arXiv:2604.20459]), variable-step OLLA, and Kalman/Wiener channel-state predictors. The authors pit Deep A2C only against a static grid-searched constant and a cold-start single-loop OLLA, setting up a false binary between naive scalar integration and deep RL.
   **What would address it:** Evaluate all comparators under steady-state conditions (ruling out transient initialization artifacts), scale OLLA step sizes proportionally during the target-BLER sweep so the loop actually tracks the target BLER, and benchmark against multi-offset OLLA [arXiv:2604.20459] and classical predictive channel filters.

### Minor concerns
- **[Evidence — cherry-picking]:** The reporting across all 12 points in Table 1 avoids cherry-picking of aggregate throughput; however, trace-level scatter plots (Figure 10) and policy entropy curves (Figures 3–4) are shown only for the headline point (+9 dB burst). It should be verified that these patterns hold across +6 dB and +12 dB regimes.
- **[Evidence — pre-registration vs post-hoc]:** While the initial 9-point sweep was pre-registered, the subsequent Tier-1 iterations (`tier1`, `tier1b`, `tier1-nobc`) are post-hoc diagnostic interventions. The manuscript must strictly demarcate the confirmatory pre-registered null from the exploratory diagnostic iterations.
- **[Evidence — ablations]:** The paper lacks an ablation of the OLLA-relative action encoding itself. Does constraining the action space to relative offsets around OLLA bias the agent toward collapsing to an offset of zero or a constant, compared to direct MCS selection?
- **[Evidence — quality vs quantity]:** The manuscript provides 14 figures and multiple tables, but many figures (Figures 1, 2, 5, 10, 11) repeatedly display the same 30-trace aggregate throughput across different visualizations without adding independent evidential depth.
- **[Limitations]:** While the paper is remarkably candid about its primary pre-registered null, it treats the steady-state `Warm OLLA` failure as a nuisance variable rather than acknowledging it as a critical operational limitation of the RL agent.
- **[Presentation]:** In Section 2.1 (Equation 1), the text defines $\Delta_{comb} = 2.5\text{ dB}$, but fails to specify whether combining gain is capped at a maximum SNR or bounded by coding rate limits. In Section 4.1, Table 1 column headers lack units for margins and $p$-values.

### Belief update
- **Prior belief:** Deep reinforcement learning link-adaptation algorithms in wireless cellular networks outperform classical OLLA loops primarily because neural networks learn non-linear, state-conditional policies that dynamically track fast-fading channel variations and interference bursts.
- **Updated belief:** The throughput gain of RL over standard OLLA in environments with HARQ chase combining is largely an operating-setpoint artifact (shifting the first-transmission BLER target from the conservative 10% standard to a throughput-maximizing 40–45%), which requires zero state-conditional adaptivity and can be matched by a simple static constant offset. My probability that published "RL for link adaptation" papers are observing setpoint shifts rather than dynamic channel tracking increased significantly (from ~0.35 to ~0.75).
- **Residual skepticism:** The paper does not shift my belief that state-conditional adaptivity is useless in wireless link adaptation. Because Deep A2C was evaluated without recurrent memory on a delayed, noisy POMDP, and because steady-state OLLA defeated Deep A2C, the null result reflects the failure of a specific, undertrained feedforward RL architecture rather than a fundamental physical property of the wireless channel.

### Verdict
**Major revision** — The paper provides valuable empirical transparency by documenting a pre-registered null result and exposing HARQ setpoint shifts, but its core claim that state-conditional adaptivity has no value rests on under-tuned and mischaracterized baselines—most critically evidenced by the fact that steady-state OLLA actually defeats the RL agent ($p = 0.0003$).
